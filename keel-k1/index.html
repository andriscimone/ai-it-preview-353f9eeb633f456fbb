The brief

Keep what makes a motorcycle good, remove what kills its riders

A motorcycle is narrow, light and cheap to run. It filters through traffic and parks anywhere. It also leaves the rider exposed to weather and to every impact, it falls over when it stops, and it takes skill to ride well.

Per mile travelled, US motorcyclists die at almost 28 times the rate of passenger-car occupants.NHTSA Traffic Safety Facts, 2023 data

The K-1 keeps the narrow track and the lean, then adds a crash structure, seat belts, a roof and a balance system that does not depend on the rider’s feet. The same sensors and computer that make it safer also let it drive on its own, which turns a two-wheeler into something you can summon, send to park, or ride as a passenger.

Balance

How it stays upright

Two flywheels spin at up to 24,000 rpm under the floor. Tilting a spinning flywheel produces a torque at right angles to the tilt, so tilting the pair fore and aft pushes the vehicle left or right. That is a control moment gyroscope, the same device that points the International Space Station.

At a standstill

The gyros do all the work. Each unit stores 261 N·m·s of angular momentum and can deliver about 910 N·m of roll torque. A fully loaded K-1 that is knocked 15° off vertical needs about 770 N·m to come back, so one unit is enough.

Gravity torque = m · g · h · sin θ = 523 kg × 9.81 × 0.58 m × sin θ

Moving

Above about 50 km/h the K-1 balances the way a motorcycle does, by steering under its centre of mass. The gyros stay spinning to soak up gusts, potholes and side contact. A 25 m/s crosswind gust pushes with about 830 N·m, inside one unit’s capacity.

If something fails

The two gyro units, their power feeds and their controllers are independent. If both fault, or the vehicle loses power, two landing legs swing out in 0.3 s. The same legs hold it up when parked, so the flywheels can spin down.

In a corner the whole cabin leans, like an aircraft banking. The lean angle is chosen so that the combined pull of gravity and cornering points straight down through the seat:

tan θ = v² / (g · r)  →  70 km/h on a 60 m curve leans 33°

Occupants feel heavier for a moment but are never pushed sideways, which is why a passenger who has never ridden a motorcycle can sit in the back without bracing.

Energy

Range from first principles

An enclosed, narrow body has far less drag than a rider sitting upright in the wind. That is where most of the range comes from, not the battery.

Model inputs, chosen on the cautious side:

  • Drag area 0.24 m² (Cd 0.26 × 0.92 m² frontal area). A rider on a faired sport bike is typically twice that.
  • Rolling resistance 0.009, test mass 423 kg (curb plus one 85 kg occupant).
  • 88% battery-to-wheel efficiency.
  • 600 W always-on load for climate, computer, sensors and gyros; 800 W in town.
  • City cycle: 50 km/h peaks, two stops per km, 70% of braking energy recovered.
  • 28 kWh usable.

For scale: the Peraves E-Tracer, an enclosed two-wheeler that weighed 651 kg, won the 2010 Automotive X Prize tandem class at 205.3 MPGe, about 102 Wh/km from the wall. The K-1’s mixed figure works out to about 59 Wh/km from the wall, or roughly 355 MPGe.
